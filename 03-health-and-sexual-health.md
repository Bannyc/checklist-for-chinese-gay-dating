# 03 - Health and Sexual Health

Health conversations are about **mutual care and informed choices**, not judgment. Everyone, regardless of orientation or status, benefits from knowing their own health picture and sharing what's relevant at the right time.

> **Disclaimer:** This file is general information, not medical advice. Consult a licensed doctor or a reputable community health organization for personal guidance. Guidelines and availability of services change; verify locally.

*Suggested timing: sexual health basics before any sexual contact; broader health and long-term topics in the developing stage and before committing.*

---

## Guiding principles

1. **Test together, not as an accusation.** "Let's both get tested" is care, not distrust.
2. **A person with a manageable health condition can be a great partner.** What matters is honesty, treatment, and mutual informed decisions.
3. **No one owes you their full medical history on a first date**, but you have the right to protect yourself, and they have the right to privacy. Sexual health basics should be discussed *before* sex.
4. **Never share someone's health information without their consent.**
5. **Stigma harms everyone.** Avoid language like "clean" or "dirty." Prefer "negative/positive", "tested recently", "on treatment".

---

## A. Sexual health conversation checklist

**Before sexual contact, talk about:**
- [ ] When were each of you last tested for HIV and other STIs?
- [ ] What protection do you each use (condoms, lubricant, PrEP)?
- [ ] Are there any current symptoms or recent exposures?
- [ ] What are each person's boundaries and comfort levels?
- [ ] Have you each had other partners recently? Are you currently seeing anyone else?
- [ ] How will you handle it if one of you has a new partner or an exposure?

**Sample phrases**
- "I get tested regularly. When was your last test?"
- "I'd feel more comfortable if we both got checked before we stop using condoms."
- "Are you on PrEP, or is that something you've thought about?"
- "If anything changes with your health or partners, can we promise to tell each other?"

## B. Recommended testing and prevention (discuss with a doctor)

Common items people discuss with healthcare providers. Exact recommendations depend on individual risk and local services.

- [ ] **HIV** test (regular testing is often recommended for sexually active people; frequency depends on risk)
- [ ] **Syphilis** test
- [ ] **Gonorrhea and chlamydia** (can be in the throat, rectum, or urethra, so ask about site-specific testing)
- [ ] **Hepatitis B** status and vaccination (and hepatitis A vaccination, if recommended)
- [ ] **Hepatitis C** screening, if relevant to history
- [ ] **HPV** (ask a doctor about vaccination options and anal/oral health)
- [ ] **PrEP** (pre-exposure prophylaxis) awareness and availability
- [ ] **PEP** (post-exposure prophylaxis): know it exists and that it must be started quickly after a possible exposure (typically within 72 hours); learn where to get it in your city *before* you need it
- [ ] Mental health check-in when needed

## C. HIV and "U=U": informed, non-stigmatizing approach

- **PrEP, condoms, and treatment all reduce risk.** Many people use a combination.
- **U=U (Undetectable = Untransmittable):** According to major scientific and public-health consensus, a person living with HIV who is on effective treatment and maintains an undetectable viral load does not sexually transmit HIV to partners. Verify the details with a medical professional.
- If someone discloses that they are HIV-positive, the most important questions are about **trust, honesty, and care**, not shock or rejection. It's okay to take time, ask a doctor, and decide what you're comfortable with. It is not okay to expose, mock, or spread their status.
- If *you* are living with HIV, you deserve respect and love. Disclose when you are ready and safe to do so, and prioritize your safety if you worry about a negative reaction.

## D. Substance use and risk behaviors

- [ ] Alcohol: how much and how often? Is it affecting health, safety, or behavior?
- [ ] Tobacco/vaping: compatible with your preferences?
- [ ] Drugs: Note that in mainland China, illegal drug use carries serious legal consequences, and drug use during sex ("chemsex") can raise health, consent, and safety risks. Be honest about your boundaries.
- [ ] Do they respect your "no" regarding substances?
- [ ] Is there any pattern of risky behavior (unprotected sex with unknown partners, driving after drinking, etc.) that makes you uncomfortable?

> If you or a partner struggle with substances, seek professional or community support rather than trying to manage it alone.

## E. General physical health

Not a medical exam, just things couples reasonably share over time:

- [ ] Chronic conditions that affect daily life or long-term plans (diabetes, hypertension, hepatitis B carrier status, autoimmune conditions, etc.)
- [ ] Medications they take regularly
- [ ] Allergies (food, medication), especially if you'll eat or travel together
- [ ] Sleep, diet, and exercise habits: compatible rhythms?
- [ ] Family medical history that may matter later
- [ ] Do they see doctors when needed, or avoid healthcare out of fear/shame?
- [ ] Health insurance situation

## F. Mental and emotional health

- [ ] Any history of depression, anxiety, trauma, or other conditions they feel comfortable sharing?
- [ ] Are they receiving or open to counseling?
- [ ] Have they experienced conversion-type pressure (forced "treatment", family attempts to change orientation)? This can leave lasting impact; be gentle.
- [ ] How do they manage burnout, work pressure (e.g., long working hours), and family stress?
- [ ] Any history of self-harm or crisis? If a partner is in crisis, encourage professional help and involve trusted people. You are not responsible for being their only support.

## G. Body image and sexual compatibility

- [ ] Do you share a similar view of intimacy frequency and style?
- [ ] Can you talk about preferences and limits without shame?
- [ ] Do you each feel safe saying "not tonight", or "let's stop"?
- [ ] Are role preferences (if any) and boundaries discussed respectfully, without stereotypes?
- [ ] Do you respect each other's bodies regardless of size, appearance, or ability?

## H. What to do after possible exposure

1. Stay calm; seek care promptly.
2. Contact a hospital infectious-disease department, local CDC center, or a community health organization for PEP and testing guidance.
3. Tell relevant partners honestly so they can protect themselves.
4. Avoid blame; focus on health and next steps.

## I. Where to find support (verify locally)

- Local CDC (疾病预防控制中心) offices often provide HIV counseling and testing; services and privacy practices vary by area.
- Community-based LGBT health organizations in various cities provide testing, PrEP guidance, and peer support. Search for reputable organizations in your city and check recent reviews or recommendations from trusted community members.
- Public hospitals' infectious disease or dermatology/venereology departments for STI care.
- Mental health services: hospitals' psychiatry departments, university counseling centers, or reputable counseling providers.

> Always confirm opening hours, privacy options (anonymous vs. real-name testing), and costs before going.
